## 2.2 Educational recommendation and learner agency

Educational recommender research addresses different tasks. Learning-resource retrieval serves an instructional need, whereas programme or career guidance may inform choices involving prerequisites, time and cost. The latter consequences are contextual reasoning here, not findings from the recommender studies cited below. An item-ranking result in one setting therefore cannot establish the suitability of a study route in another. Deschênes’ (2020) systematic review distinguishes recommendation accuracy, learner satisfaction and learning-performance effects. Its 56 included studies concern learning contexts; it deliberately excludes learning-path recommendations, so it cautions against equating retrieval with learner benefit but does not establish how Sri Lankan learners should choose qualifications. Da Silva et al. (2023) also review teaching-and-learning recommenders. Their publisher abstract reports 16 included studies from 756 screened records, frequent use of hybrid methods and limited attention to pedagogical effectiveness. Since only the abstract and metadata were examined for that review, these aggregate findings do not support finer claims about study quality or individual algorithms.

Lahoud et al. (2023) are a closer, though non-equivalent, comparator: their Lebanese case study compares five approaches to university-major and career-domain guidance. They report 869 collected graduate profiles and 20,000 course ratings, then describe separate experimental subsets, including 469 cases for collaborative filtering and a 658-case filtered base for an ontology/case-based approach. These counts refer to different stages and experiments; they should not be added into one sample. The study reports algorithmic measures and feedback from high-school students, university students and graduates. Similarity, personalisation, usefulness and satisfaction measures do not establish formal admission correctness or directly benchmark WiseLanka’s rankings. The study shows that domain knowledge has been applied to programme and career guidance, but its Lebanese context, data and design do not establish local effectiveness in Sri Lanka.

Taken together, these studies justify a bounded research question rather than a claim that a particular recommender is generally effective. Learning-resource studies can inform distinctions among algorithmic and learner outcomes; programme/career studies can inform the handling of structured domain knowledge and alternative methods. Neither evidence group establishes current Sri Lankan learner needs or the accuracy of WiseLanka’s curated records. The National Education Commission’s Sri Lankan career-guidance report is relevant historical context, but its date and study scope do not make it a current estimate of demand across A/L, vocational and early-career groups (National Education Commission, 2016). This review therefore treats local transfer as an empirical and source-validation issue: official requirements must be checked at the applicable provider and intake, while usability and perceived relevance require separate evaluation with eligible users. This is an interpretation for WiseLanka, not a finding reported by those sources.

## 2.3 Explicit constraints and hybrid recommendation

Recommendation methods address different evidence conditions. Constraint-based recommenders represent explicit user requirements and domain constraints; this is useful conceptually when a candidate must satisfy conditions, rather than merely resemble a preference description (Felfernig et al., 2015). Burke’s (2002) taxonomy also shows that “hybrid” covers multiple combinations, whose value depends on the component methods and task. Its empirical work concerns a restaurant recommender and historical interaction logs, so it supplies a vocabulary for combination strategies rather than evidence that an education-guidance hybrid will perform better. A policy-aware system must additionally distinguish a sound rule engine from sound underlying policy data: an accurately executed rule can still be stale, incomplete or misrepresented if its source is wrong.

This distinction motivates separating formal eligibility from recommendation relevance. WiseLanka’s implementation assigns `MET` when available evidence satisfies a documented rule, `NOT_MET` when evidence demonstrates an unmet rule, and `NOT_ASSESSED` when the available information is insufficient. Its content-similarity ranker orders exploration within applicable academic assessment groups; a relevance score cannot compensate for an unmet or unassessed requirement. These states and their ordering are WiseLanka design decisions, not outcomes established by Felfernig or Burke. Treating a missing grade or requirement as unknown rather than as failure is likewise a conservative system-design inference. The approach requires source-level rule review and explanation-comprehension evaluation; the cited recommender literature does not validate its educational correctness.

The ranking alternatives rest on different mechanisms. Rules expose explicit conditions but do not themselves interpret varied expressions of interest. Salton and Buckley (1988) examine term-weighting schemes combining term frequency, inverse document frequency and normalisation in vector retrieval; this supports a lexical baseline, not WiseLanka’s exact smoothed-IDF formula. SBERT uses siamese and triplet structures to produce sentence embeddings compared by cosine similarity (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019). Its evaluations concern semantic-similarity and transfer tasks, not programme suitability, and the paper reports that performance depends on task and domain. MiniLM’s contribution is self-attention distillation for Transformer compression (Wang et al., 2020); it does not evaluate the deployed sentence model or educational recommendations. WiseLanka’s frozen semantic similarity is therefore a candidate relevance signal, not an admission or success probability. Comparing deterministic, TF–IDF and semantic rankings on identical eligible candidate sets can show ordering and runtime differences; without independent relevance judgements, it cannot show which is better.

For Sri Lankan pathways, the constraint catalogue has a further dependency that generic recommender studies do not resolve: the authority, date and applicability of each requirement. A current official source may state minimum academic criteria while leaving selection, equivalence or progression to a provider. The system should expose those conditions and source links, and should not imply guaranteed admission. Where evidence is absent or conflicting, abstention is more defensible than completing the rule by assumption. This is a design rationale for the prototype, not proof that its current dataset covers all routes or that explanations are understood. The local knowledge base and rule execution must be evaluated separately from ranking relevance, and both must remain distinct from learner satisfaction or educational outcomes.
